\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Vehicles increasingly exchange information with each other to support services such as collision
warnings and cooperative traffic management. Links between nearby vehicles, known as \ac{V2V} links,
carry this traffic directly. They need only moderate throughput, but they must deliver packets with low
delay and high reliability \cite{li+22fedmarl}. In cellular vehicular networks, a common
way to provide these links without dedicated spectrum is to let \ac{V2V} pairs reuse the channels already
assigned to \acp{CUE}. Reuse makes better use of a scarce resource, but it creates mutual interference: a
\ac{V2V} transmitter that picks a crowded channel, or transmits with too much power, lowers the rate of the
cellular user on that channel and can make its own packets, or those of other pairs, fail
\cite{li+22fedmarl}.

Deciding which channel each pair reuses and how much power it uses is hard for two reasons. First,
vehicles move fast, so channel conditions and the network topology change quickly. Tracking the
instantaneous channel state of every link at a central controller is impractical and costs substantial
signaling \cite{li+22fedmarl}. Second, decisions made now affect performance later. A pair that stays
silent causes no interference, but packets keep arriving and its queue grows until it has to transmit
anyway. Resource allocation in this setting is therefore a sequential decision problem
rather than a one-shot optimization, which makes it a natural fit for \ac{RL} \cite{li+22fedmarl}.

In this setting, each \ac{V2V} pair can be treated as an agent that chooses its channel and power from what
it can observe locally. This turns the problem into one of \ac{MARL}, which inherits the
well-known difficulties of that setting \cite{gronauer+22marlsurvey,nguyen+20madrl}. Each agent sees only
part of the network. Because all agents learn at the same time, the environment looks non-stationary from
the point of view of any single one of them. And the joint state and action spaces grow exponentially with
the number of agents, so a method that works for a handful of pairs may not work on a dense road.

One way for agents to benefit from each other's experience without pooling their data is \ac{FL}. In its
standard form, \ac{FedAvg}, each client trains a copy of a shared model on its own data, and a central
server periodically averages the clients' parameters into a global model that it sends back to them
\cite{mcmahan+17fedavg}. \citet{li+22fedmarl} applied this idea to \ac{V2V} resource allocation. In their
scheme, every pair runs a \ac{D3QN} agent on its local observations, and the \ac{BS} periodically averages
the agents' networks with \ac{FedAvg} and broadcasts the result. Sharing the averaged model gives each
agent implicit knowledge of the parts of the network it cannot observe, and it avoids the communication
overhead and privacy concerns of collecting every vehicle's data for centralized training. In their
simulations, the scheme outperformed its baselines in both the cellular sum rate and the \ac{V2V} packet
delivery rate \cite{li+22fedmarl}.

This design, however, puts the \ac{BS} at the center of training. The agents make their decisions on their own,
precisely to avoid the extra delay of going through the cellular infrastructure, but they cannot learn
without it. In every round, the \ac{BS} collects all local models, averages them, and sends the result
back. It also evaluates the global reward that every agent learns from and broadcasts it at the end of
each slot \cite{li+22fedmarl}. A central server of this kind is a single point of failure, concentrates
the communication load on one node, and is vulnerable to attacks \cite{ge+24dfl}. In a vehicular
network, these costs are concrete: all model traffic goes through the cellular uplink and downlink, and a
vehicle that loses contact with the \ac{BS} also drops out of training.

In \ac{DFL}, the server is removed altogether. Devices connect to each other directly, and each one combines the
knowledge of a few neighbors, so that what one device learns spreads through the network without a
coordinator \cite{ge+24dfl}. In the \ac{MARL} literature, the analogous setting, in which agents cooperate
through peer-to-peer (gossip) communication instead of a central node, is studied as decentralized
\ac{MARL} \cite{cheruiyot+25marlsurvey}. The vehicular setting seems well suited to this approach. In the
scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl}, each pair already relies on information about the pairs within its
maximum communication radius of 300~m, which it obtains by radio sensing or over the sidelink. Those same
neighbors could exchange model parameters directly, and as the positions of the vehicles change, so do
the neighborhoods through which knowledge travels.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

The scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl} makes resource allocation decentralized at execution time but keeps
training centralized at the \ac{BS}. This thesis asks whether that central aggregator is necessary.
Specifically, we study the following question:

\begin{quote}
  \itshape Can the \ac{BS} be removed from model aggregation in federated \ac{MARL} for \ac{V2V} resource
  allocation, by letting each vehicle average its model with those of nearby vehicles, without losing
  performance, and at what communication cost?
\end{quote}

By performance we mean the quantities the original scheme optimizes: the rate of the \acp{CUE} and the
delay and reliability of the \ac{V2V} links, all of which enter the reward. By communication cost we mean
how many model transfers each scheme needs and how those transfers are distributed among the nodes. A
scheme in which every transfer goes through a single node is harder to scale than one that spreads the
load.

% Si --local-reward queda como opcion por defecto, este parrafo cambia.
We limit the scope of this thesis to model aggregation. The \ac{BS} of \citet{li+22fedmarl} also computes
the global reward shared by all agents. We keep that reward unchanged, so that any difference we observe
comes from the aggregation scheme and not from a different learning objective, and leave the
decentralization of the reward for future work.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The general objective of this thesis is to design and evaluate a decentralized aggregation scheme for
federated \ac{MARL} that removes the \ac{BS} from model aggregation in \ac{V2V} resource allocation. The
specific objectives are the following:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Reimplement the scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl} as a centralized baseline, together with the
    reference methods needed to interpret its results: agents that learn without ever sharing their
    models, and heuristics that do not learn at all.
  \item Design an aggregation scheme in which each agent periodically averages its model with those of
    the \ac{V2V} pairs within its communication range, without involving the \ac{BS}.
  \item Evaluate both schemes as the number of \ac{V2V} pairs grows from 5 to 30, in terms of reward,
    \ac{CUE} rate, \ac{V2V} delay and outage, convergence speed, and communication cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A reimplementation of the scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl} as a multi-agent simulation environment
    with \ac{D3QN} agents and \ac{FedAvg} aggregation. The implementation also includes independent
    learners that never share their models, which correspond to \ac{DTE} \cite{amato24cooperative}, and
    two heuristics that do not learn. Each equation of the system model is checked against the code.
  % Actualizar cuando terminen los sets de diagnostico recompensa y gamma.
  \item An analysis of a failure found during the replication. In our implementation, the learning agents
    lose part of what they learned as exploration decays, and for up to 20 pairs they end below a simple
    greedy heuristic. Our diagnosis points to the timing of the reward: staying silent is rewarded immediately,
    while its cost, a queue that eventually overflows, shows up much later. A fixed rule that uses only
    local information, transmitting at full power when the pair's own link is strong and otherwise
    waiting until its queue is nearly full, outperforms the greedy heuristic for every number of pairs
    we tested.
  % resultado principal cuando terminen los jobs de gossip.
  \item A neighbor-based aggregation scheme for federated \ac{MARL}, in which vehicles average their
    models with the pairs in their communication range, and its comparison with centralized \ac{FedAvg}
    and with independent learners in terms of reward and communication cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Document Structure}
\label{sec:document-structure}

The rest of this document is organized as follows. \fref{chap:background} introduces the concepts the
thesis builds on: \ac{RL} and the \ac{D3QN} agent, \ac{MARL}, \ac{FL}, and \ac{V2V} communications.
\fref{chap:related-work} reviews work on learning-based resource allocation for vehicular networks,
federated and decentralized \ac{MARL}, and the scalability of multi-agent learning, and positions this
thesis among them. \fref{chap:system-model} describes the network scenario, the channel model, and the
formulation of the problem as a multi-agent decision process. \fref{chap:decentralized-aggregation}
presents the agents and the two aggregation schemes, centralized \ac{FedAvg} and neighbor-based gossip,
and analyzes their communication cost. \fref{chap:experimental-setup} describes the simulation
parameters, baselines, metrics, and training protocol. \fref{chap:results} reports the replication of the
baseline, its behavior as the number of agents grows, the diagnosis of the learners' collapse, and the
comparison between decentralized and centralized aggregation. Finally, \fref{chap:conclusions}
summarizes the findings, discusses the limitations of this work, and outlines future work.
