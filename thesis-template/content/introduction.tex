\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

This thesis is about how many vehicles can learn to share a scarce resource: the radio channels on which
they communicate. Each pair of vehicles that talk to each other decides, again and again, on which channel
to transmit and with how much power. If all the pairs choose well, their messages arrive on time and the
cellular network, which owns those channels, is barely affected. If they choose badly, they disturb each
other and take capacity away from the cellular network. The thesis proposes and evaluates two changes to a
recent learning method for this problem: a way to share what the vehicles learn that does not go through
the base station of the cellular network, and a way to reward each vehicle for what it did itself.

\section{Motivation}
% Por qué importan los enlaces V2V (avisos de seguridad con poco retardo y alta confiabilidad), cómo reutilizan los canales de la red celular y por qué elegir canal y potencia es un problema difícil que se ha abordado con MARL federado Li et al
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Modern vehicles exchange information to warn of sudden braking, to prevent collisions, and to coordinate
traffic. When two nearby vehicles communicate directly, without going through an antenna, they form a
\ac{V2V} link. These links do not need to carry much data, but they must deliver it with little delay and
high reliability: a braking warning that arrives late is useless. The \ac{3GPP}, for example, sets a maximum
latency of \SI{100}{\milli\second} for these messages \cite{3gpp16v2x}.

Radio spectrum is scarce and expensive. A common way to serve \ac{V2V} links without giving them channels of
their own is to let them \emph{reuse} the channels that the cellular network has already assigned to its
users, the \acp{CUE} \cite{liang+19spectrum,li+22fedmarl}. Reuse makes better use of the spectrum, but it
creates interference in both directions. A vehicle that transmits on the channel of a cellular user lowers
the quality of the signal that this user sends to the \ac{BS}, and the signal of the cellular user, in turn,
disturbs the receiver of the vehicle. If two pairs of vehicles choose the same channel, they also interfere
with each other.

Deciding which channel each pair reuses, and with what power it transmits, is hard for two reasons. The
first is that the environment changes quickly. Vehicles move, radio conditions vary from one millisecond to
the next, and with them changes which choice is good. A central controller that wanted to decide for
everyone would need to know the state of every link at every instant, which requires a lot of control
signaling \cite{li+22fedmarl}. The second reason is that decisions made now affect what happens later. A pair
that stops transmitting does not interfere with anyone, but the data it must send keep piling up in its
\emph{queue}, and sooner or later it will have to send them in a hurry. The problem is therefore a sequence
of linked decisions, not a one-step optimization.

This kind of problem is what \ac{RL} studies: an agent learns to decide by trying actions and observing the
rewards it receives \cite{sutton+18rl}. When each pair of vehicles is an agent that decides with what it
observes around it, the problem becomes one of \ac{MARL}, with the difficulties of that field
\cite{gronauer+22marlsurvey,nguyen+20madrl}. Each agent sees only part of the network. Since all of them
learn at the same time, the environment that each one sees keeps changing, because the others also change
how they act. And the number of possible combinations of actions grows exponentially with the number of
agents.

One way for the agents to benefit from the experience of the others, without gathering their data in one
place, is \ac{FL}. In its best-known form, \ac{FedAvg}, each participant trains its own copy of a model with
its own data, and a central server periodically averages the parameters of all the copies and sends the
average back \cite{mcmahan+17fedavg}. \citet{li+22fedmarl} applied this idea to \ac{V2V} resource
allocation. In their scheme, each pair of vehicles is an agent with a neural network that learns from its
own observations, and the \ac{BS} of the cell periodically averages the networks of all the agents and
sends them the result. In addition, the \ac{BS} computes at every instant a single reward, which measures
the performance of the whole cell, and sends it to all the agents. In their simulations, this scheme
outperformed their reference methods both in the rate of the \acp{CUE} and in the delay of the \ac{V2V}
links \cite{li+22fedmarl}.

This design puts the \ac{BS} at the center of learning. The vehicles decide on their own, precisely so as
not to depend on the cellular infrastructure, but they cannot learn without it. In every round, the \ac{BS}
receives all the models, averages them, and sends the result back, and at every instant it computes and
broadcasts the reward. A central server of this kind is a single point of failure, concentrates the whole
communication load on one node, and must be trusted by every participant \cite{kairouz+21advances,ge+24dfl}.
In a vehicular network, these costs are concrete: all the model traffic goes through the cellular network,
and a vehicle that loses contact with the \ac{BS} stops benefiting from what the others learn.

\Ac{DFL} removes the central server. The participants connect directly to each other, and each one combines
what a few neighbors have learned, so that knowledge spreads through the network without a coordinator
\cite{lian+17dpsgd,hegedus+19gossip,ge+24dfl}. The vehicular setting seems made for this idea. In the scheme
of \citet{li+22fedmarl}, each pair already uses information from the pairs within \SI{300}{\meter}, its
maximum communication range. Those same neighbors could exchange their models directly, and since vehicles
move, the groups through which knowledge flows would change too. \citet{zhang+20v2xfed} already average
models only within groups of nearby pairs, but it is still the \ac{BS} that forms the groups and does the
averaging.

A second choice of the original scheme also deserves a second look: the shared reward. When all the agents
receive the same number, none of them can tell which part of that number it caused and which part the
others caused. This is the problem of \termdef{credit assignment}, one of the central challenges of
multi-agent learning \cite{wolpert+01collectives,agogino+08rewards,gronauer+22marlsurvey}. It gets worse as
the number of agents grows, because the contribution of each one is diluted among those of all the others.

\section{Problem statement}
% El esquema de Li et al. decide de forma descentralizada pero aprende a través de la BS (FedAvg y recompensa compartida). Preguntas de investigación: PI1, quitar la BS de la agregación de modelos; PI2, usar una recompensa individual por agente.
\label{sec:intro-problem}

The scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl} is decentralized when it decides, but centralized when it learns. The
\ac{BS} takes part at two points: it averages the models of all the agents with \ac{FedAvg}, and it computes
the shared reward from which all of them learn. This thesis asks whether these two pieces are necessary,
through two research questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] Can the \ac{BS} be removed from the aggregation of models, by having each vehicle average its
    model with those of nearby vehicles, without losing performance? How much communication does this cost,
    and how is the load spread across the nodes of the network?
  \item[RQ2.] Do the agents learn better if each one receives an individual reward, which measures what its
    own action can change, instead of the reward shared by all?
\end{description}

By \emph{performance} we mean what the original scheme measures: the rate of the \acp{CUE} and the delay and
reliability of the \ac{V2V} links, which are combined into a single number, the shared reward of
\citet{li+22fedmarl} (Section~\ref{sec:sm-shared-reward}). To keep the comparison fair, every method is
judged by that same reward, even when its agents learn from another one. By \emph{communication cost} we
mean how many models are transferred between nodes, and in how many of those transfers the busiest node
takes part (Section~\ref{sec:pa-comm-cost}). A scheme in which everything goes through a single node scales
worse than one that spreads the load.

\section{Objectives}
% Objetivo general (diseñar, implementar y evaluar la arquitectura descentralizada con recompensa individual) y lista de objetivos específicos.
\label{sec:intro-objectives}

The general objective of this thesis is to design, implement, and evaluate a decentralized federated
learning architecture for multi-agent reinforcement learning applied to \ac{V2V} resource allocation, which
removes the \ac{BS} from the aggregation of models, together with an individual reward for each agent. The
specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Implement the scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl} (the simulation environment, the agents, and the
    aggregation with \ac{FedAvg}), together with the reference methods needed to interpret its results:
    agents that learn without sharing anything, and fixed rules that do not learn. Check every equation of
    the model with automated tests.
  \item Design an aggregation by gossip, in which each agent averages its model with those of the pairs
    within its communication range, without the \ac{BS}, and analyze its communication cost.
  \item Design an individual reward for each agent and compare it with the shared reward.
  \item Evaluate the combinations of aggregation and reward with 5 to 30 \ac{V2V} pairs, in terms of reward,
    quality of service, learning speed, similarity between the models of the agents, and communication
    cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
% Las tres contribuciones: implementación verificada y reproducible del esquema de Li et al., agregación descentralizada por gossip entre vecinos y recompensa individual por agente.
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

This thesis makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A tested and reproducible implementation of the scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl}.} It includes
    a multi-agent simulation environment with the channel, queue, and reward models of the paper, simplified
    where needed; \ac{D3QN} agents with \ac{PER}; three forms of aggregation (\ac{FedAvg}, gossip, and none);
    and two fixed reference rules. It is checked by 191 automated tests, and a script generates the tables
    and figures of the results directly from the logs of the runs, without copying anything by hand. The
    evaluation comprises 216 runs of 2000 episodes, about 800 hours of computation
    (Section~\ref{sec:exp-runs}). It reproduces part of the behavior of the published scheme: with the Greedy rule, the rate of the
    \acp{CUE} falls by 65\% from 5 to 30 pairs, against 70\% in the paper, and learning protects the
    \acp{CUE} much better. It does not reproduce the absolute values, the delay, or, with the shared reward,
    the fall in the rate of the \acp{CUE} (Section~\ref{sec:res-replication}).

  \item \textbf{A decentralized architecture for federated learning among neighboring vehicles.} Each agent
    averages its network with those of the pairs within \SI{300}{\meter}, the same neighborhood that its
    observation already uses, without going through the \ac{BS}. The groups of neighbors change with the
    positions of the vehicles, and a variant limits to $S$ the number of neighbors per round to bound the
    cost (Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-architecture}). With the individual reward, this aggregation beats
    \ac{FedAvg} in all 12 paired comparisons with 10 to 25 pairs, by 459 to 1754 points of reward per
    episode, and in 14 of 18 over all six numbers of pairs (Section~\ref{sec:res-gossip}). The variant with
    $S = 2$ beats \ac{FedAvg} in 16 of 18 comparisons, including every seed from 10 to 30 pairs, never
    transfers more models than \ac{FedAvg}, and its busiest vehicle takes part in 10\% to 34\% of the
    transfers that the \ac{BS} handles in \ac{FedAvg} (10\% to 26\% from 10 pairs on)
    (Section~\ref{sec:res-communication}).

  \item \textbf{An individual reward that works better than the shared reward.} Each agent is judged by its
    own queue, by the reliability of its own link, and by the \ac{CUE} of the channel it chose
    (Section~\ref{sec:pa-individual-reward}). Measured with the shared reward of all the agents, it improves
    the result in 50 of 54 paired comparisons, and in all 18 with gossip, by 244 to 3726 points. With the
    shared reward, by contrast, the agents learn to stay silent almost all the time as the number of pairs
    grows (Section~\ref{sec:res-reward}).
\end{enumerate}

The two proposals reinforce each other. Without the individual reward, gossip does not consistently beat
\ac{FedAvg}: it wins 10 of 18 comparisons. With it, gossip wins in the whole middle range of the number of
pairs. Together, they beat our implementation of the original scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl}, that is,
\ac{FedAvg} with the shared reward, in all 18 paired comparisons: three seeds for each of the six sizes of
the network (Section~\ref{sec:res-combined}).

These gains have a limit. A fixed rule that uses only local information, the strength of the pair's own link
and its queue, beats every learning method, including the proposal (Section~\ref{sec:res-threshold}). The
contributions improve learning compared with the original scheme, but they do not close the gap to what that
information allows.

\section{Scope}
% Límites del trabajo: todo es simulación, una sola celda con 15 CUE y 5 a 30 pares V2V, simplificaciones frente al artículo original y posiciones fijas dentro de cada episodio.
\label{sec:intro-scope}

All the results of this thesis come from simulation. Section~\ref{sec:sm-scenario} describes the scenario;
here we only summarize where it simplifies the model of \citet{li+22fedmarl}: the vehicles are placed
uniformly in the cell instead of on roads, the channel has a single path-loss law, and the traffic is
constant. The positions of the vehicles change from one episode to the next, but not within an episode. The
models are exchanged between nodes instantly, without errors, and without using the spectrum that the
agents share. The individual reward is not completely local: its term for the \ac{CUE} of the chosen channel
needs the \ac{BS} to broadcast the rate of that user. The comparisons with the original scheme are
comparisons with our implementation of it, which differs from the paper in several training choices. Each
configuration was run with three seeds. Sections~\ref{sec:res-threats} and~\ref{sec:conc-limitations}
discuss these limitations.

\section{Document structure}
% Un párrafo que resume qué contiene cada capítulo y cada apéndice.
\label{sec:intro-structure}

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} presents the basic concepts of vehicular communications, reinforcement
learning, multi-agent learning, and federated learning. Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} reviews the related
work and places this thesis with respect to it. Chapter~\ref{chap:system-model} describes the scenario, the
channel model, the queues, and the reward, and formulates the problem as a multi-agent decision problem.
Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-architecture} presents the agents, the centralized aggregation with \ac{FedAvg},
the decentralized aggregation by gossip, the individual reward, and the communication cost of each scheme.
Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental-setup} details the parameters, the compared methods, the metrics, and how the
methods are compared. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents and discusses the results. Finally,
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} answers the research questions and sums up the contributions, the
limitations, and the future work. The appendices gather the notation (Appendix~\ref{apx:notation}), the
diagnosis of training and the configuration choices (Appendix~\ref{apx:diagnosis}), the detailed results
(Appendix~\ref{apx:detailed-results}), and the instructions to reproduce the experiments
(Appendix~\ref{apx:reproducibility}). Readers who already know the field can go directly from this chapter
to Chapter~\ref{chap:system-model}.
