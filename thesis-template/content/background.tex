\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter builds, from scratch, the ideas on which the rest of the thesis rests. It assumes no prior
knowledge of wireless communications or machine learning. Section~\ref{sec:bg-v2v} presents the minimum of
wireless communications needed to understand the system model: what interference is, how the quality of a
signal is measured, and how a queue of data relates to delay. Section~\ref{sec:bg-rl} explains how an agent
learns by trial and error, and Section~\ref{sec:bg-drl} shows how that learning is combined with neural
networks. Section~\ref{sec:bg-marl} moves from one learning agent to many and presents the difficulties this
brings. The last two sections present federated learning, first with a central server
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-fl}) and then without one (Section~\ref{sec:bg-dfl}).

Two reading aids appear throughout the chapter. After each equation, a list explains every symbol in it. At
the end of each main section, a box labeled \emph{Key idea} summarizes the section in a few sentences.

\section{Vehicular communications and spectrum reuse}
% Lo necesario de comunicaciones inalámbricas para entender el modelo: interferencia, calidad de una señal y relación entre cola y retardo.
\label{sec:bg-v2v}

\subsection{Vehicle-to-vehicle links and the cellular network}
% Qué son V2X y V2V, qué es un par V2V, qué mensajes lleva y cómo reutiliza los canales de subida de los CUE a costa de interferirlos.

\termdef[vehicular communications]{Vehicular communications} (\ac{V2X}) cover every way in which a vehicle
exchanges information. This thesis focuses on \ac{V2V} links, in which a vehicle sends data directly to a
nearby vehicle; each transmitter--receiver couple is a \termdef{V2V pair}. These links carry periodic safety
messages, for which timeliness matters most: the \ac{3GPP} sets their maximum latency at
\SI{100}{\milli\second} \cite{3gpp16v2x}.

A cellular network divides its spectrum into \termdef[channel]{channels}. In the \termdef{uplink}, each
\ac{CUE}, such as a phone, transmits to the \ac{BS} on its own channel. To avoid needing spectrum of their
own, \ac{V2V} pairs can reuse these uplink channels \cite{liang+19spectrum,li+22fedmarl}. The price is
\termdef{interference} (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-reuse}): the pair disturbs the \ac{CUE} at the \ac{BS}, the
\ac{CUE} disturbs the receiver of the pair, and pairs on the same channel disturb each other.

More power helps a pair's own link but hurts the others on its channel. Each pair must therefore choose a
channel and a transmit power that balance these effects; that is the decision the agents of this thesis
learn to make.

\subsection{Power and decibels}
% Escala logarítmica: razones de potencia en dB y potencias absolutas en dBm, con ejemplos de conversión.

Radio powers span many orders of magnitude, so they are written on a logarithmic scale. A power ratio $x$
in \termdef[decibel]{decibels} (dB) is $10 \log_{10} x$: multiplying a power by 10 adds \SI{10}{dB}, and
halving it subtracts about \SI{3}{dB}. An absolute power is written in \termdef{dBm}, decibels relative to
\SI{1}{\milli\watt}; the maximum transmit power in this thesis, \SI{23}{dBm}, is about
\SI{200}{\milli\watt}. In decibels, products become sums: a signal sent at \SI{23}{dBm} that loses
\SI{91}{dB} on its way arrives at $23 - 91 = \SI{-68}{dBm}$.

\subsection{Channel gain: distance and fading}
% La ganancia h = alpha * g: pérdida por distancia (gran escala) y desvanecimiento rápido (pequeña escala).

The \termdef{channel gain} $h$ is the fraction of the transmitted power that reaches the receiver: if a
transmitter sends with power $P$, the receiver gets $P\,h$. As in \citet{li+22fedmarl}, it has two factors:
\begin{equation}
  h = \alpha \, g, \qquad \alpha = \varphi \, d^{-e} \label{eq:bg-gain}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $\alpha$ is the \termdef{large-scale fading}, which falls with the distance $d$ (in meters);
    $\varphi$ is a constant and $e$ is the \termdef{path-loss exponent};
  \item $g$ is the \termdef{small-scale fading}, a random factor with mean 1 (exponentially distributed)
    that changes from one millisecond to the next because the signal reflects off its surroundings.
\end{wherelist}
With the values of this thesis ($\varphi = \SI{-68.5}{dB}$, $e = 1.61$), a receiver at \SI{25}{\meter} gets
about \SI{-91}{dB} of the transmitted power, and one at \SI{300}{\meter} about \SI{-108}{dB}. The small-scale
fading does not change this average; it makes the received power fluctuate around it.

\subsection{Signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio}
% Definición de la SINR, potencia del ruido térmico e interferencia de otros transmisores en el mismo canal.

A receiver can decode a signal if it stands out above the interference and the \termdef{noise}, a random
thermal disturbance present in every receiver. This is measured by the \ac{SINR}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SINR} = \frac{P\,h}{\sigma^2 + I} \label{eq:bg-sinr}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $P\,h$ is the useful received power;
  \item $\sigma^2$ is the noise power, \SI{-114}{dBm} for the \SI{1}{\mega\hertz} channels of this thesis
    (a density of \SI{-174}{dBm/Hz} times the bandwidth);
  \item $I$ is the sum of the powers received from the other transmitters on the same channel.
\end{wherelist}
Powers are added in milliwatts, not in dBm. For example, a pair \SI{25}{\meter} apart that transmits at
\SI{23}{dBm} receives \SI{-68}{dBm}; a \ac{CUE} on the same channel, \SI{250}{\meter} away, adds about
\SI{-84}{dBm} of interference. The \ac{SINR} is then about \SI{16}{dB}, against \SI{46}{dB} with noise alone:
in these links, interference, not noise, is the limit.

\subsection{Channel capacity}
% Fórmula de Shannon, R = W log2(1 + SINR), y cómo crece la tasa con la SINR.

The \ac{SINR} sets how many bits per second a channel can carry. \citet{shannon48communication} showed that
the highest error-free rate over a channel of bandwidth $W$ is
\begin{equation}
  R = W \log_2\bigl(1 + \mathrm{SINR}\bigr) \label{eq:bg-shannon}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $R$ is the rate in bits per second and $W$ the bandwidth in hertz;
  \item the \ac{SINR} of Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-sinr} enters as a plain ratio, not in decibels.
\end{wherelist}
The rate grows only logarithmically with the \ac{SINR}. In the example above, the interference of a single
\ac{CUE} cuts the rate of the pair from about \SI{15.3}{Mbps} to \SI{5.4}{Mbps}. As in
\citet{li+22fedmarl}, this thesis computes the rate of every link with Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-shannon}.

\subsection{Queues, delay, and Little's law}
% Cómo crece y se vacía una cola según la tasa de llegada y la de transmisión, y la ley de Little, que relaciona el tamaño de la cola con el retardo.

A \ac{V2V} transmitter stores the data it generates in a \termdef{queue} and sends them as fast as the
channel allows. If data arrive at rate $\lambda$ and the link transmits at rate $R$, the queue grows while
$R < \lambda$; a pair that stays silent ($R = 0$) only accumulates data. \termdef{Little's law}
\cite{little61queuing} links the queue to the delay:
\begin{equation}
  Q = \lambda \, D \label{eq:bg-little}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $Q$ is the average amount of data in the queue, in bits;
  \item $\lambda$ is the arrival rate, in bits per second;
  \item $D$ is the average time a bit waits in the queue, in seconds.
\end{wherelist}
Bounding the delay is therefore the same as bounding the queue. With data arriving at \SI{1}{Mbps} and a
\SI{100}{\milli\second} limit, the queue may not exceed \num{100000} bits, which a silent pair reaches after
\SI{100}{\milli\second}.

\begin{keyidea}
  When a pair of vehicles reuses the channel of a cellular user, the two disturb each other. The quality of
  each signal is measured by its \acs{SINR}, and the data rate depends on it
  (Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-shannon}). Staying silent protects the others, but it fills the queue of the silent
  pair, and a full queue means delay (Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-little}).
\end{keyidea}

\section{Reinforcement learning}
% Cómo aprende un agente por refuerzo, desde la interacción con el entorno hasta Q-learning.
\label{sec:bg-rl}

This section explains how a single agent learns to make decisions by trial and error.

\subsection{Agent, environment, and return}
% El ciclo estado, acción y recompensa, la política, el retorno con el factor de descuento gamma y, en una frase, el MDP.

In \ac{RL}, an \termdef{agent} learns to make decisions by interacting with an \termdef{environment}
\cite{sutton+18rl}. At each time step $t$, the agent observes the \termdef{state} $s^t$, chooses an
\termdef{action} $a^t$, and receives a \termdef{reward} $r^t$, a number that says how good the action was;
the environment then moves to a new state $s^{t+1}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-rl-loop}). The rule that maps
states to actions is the \termdef{policy} of the agent, $\pi$. Nobody tells the agent which action is
correct: it must find out by trying. In this thesis, each agent is a \ac{V2V} pair that observes its
channels and its queue and chooses a channel and a transmit power.

  \end{tikzpicture}

    environment answers with a new state and a reward, and the agent uses that experience to improve its
    policy.}
  \label{fig:bg-rl-loop}

The agent does not seek the best immediate reward, but the best discounted sum of rewards, called the
\termdef{return}:
\begin{equation}
  G^t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{j}\, r^{t+j} \label{eq:bg-return}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $r^{t+j}$ is the reward received $j$ steps after step $t$;
  \item $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ is the \termdef{discount factor}, which gives less weight to later rewards.
\end{wherelist}
The discount factor sets how far ahead the agent looks: roughly $1/(1-\gamma)$ steps, that is, about 100
steps with $\gamma = 0.99$ and about 10 with $\gamma = 0.9$. This choice will matter in the results.
Formally, this setting is an \ac{MDP}: the next state and the reward depend only on the current state and
action, not on the earlier history.

\subsection{Q-learning and exploration}
% La función Q, la ecuación de Bellman, la regla de Q-learning con el error TD y la exploración epsilon-greedy.

To decide, the agent needs to know how much each action is worth. The \termdef{Q function}, $Q(s, a)$, is
the expected return of taking action $a$ in state $s$ and acting well afterwards. Its optimal value
satisfies the \termdef{Bellman equation}: the value of an action is the reward it gives now plus the
discounted value of the best action in the next state. An agent that knew this function would simply
pick, in each state, the action with the largest value.

The \termdef{Q-learning} algorithm \cite{watkins+92qlearning} estimates the Q function from experience.
After each step, it moves its estimate a little toward what the Bellman equation says:
\begin{equation}
  Q(s^t, a^t) \leftarrow Q(s^t, a^t) + \eta\,\underbrace{\Bigl[\, r^t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s^{t+1}, a')
    - Q(s^t, a^t) \Bigr]}_{\text{temporal-difference error } \delta^t}
  \label{eq:bg-qlearning}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $r^t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s^{t+1}, a')$ is the \termdef{target}: what the experience of this step
    suggests the value should be;
  \item $\delta^t$ is the \termdef{temporal-difference error} (\acs{TD} error), the gap between the target
    and the current estimate;
  \item $\eta \in (0, 1]$ is the \termdef{learning rate}.
\end{wherelist}

To discover which actions are good, the agent must also try actions it does not yet believe are the best.
The usual rule is the \termdef[epsilon-greedy policy]{$\varepsilon$-greedy} policy: with probability
$\varepsilon$, the agent picks a random action, and otherwise the one with the highest estimated value.
Training starts with $\varepsilon = 1$ and lowers it gradually to a small floor.

\begin{keyidea}
  A reinforcement learning agent learns by trial and error to maximize the discounted sum of its rewards.
  The Q function says how much each action is worth in each state, and Q-learning estimates it by nudging
  it, step by step, toward the Bellman equation. The discount factor $\gamma$ sets how far ahead the agent
  looks.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Deep reinforcement learning}
% Cómo se combina el aprendizaje por refuerzo con redes neuronales y las mejoras que forman el agente D3QN.
\label{sec:bg-drl}

Q-learning stores one number per state--action pair, which is impossible when the state is made of real
numbers, such as channel gains. This section shows how a neural network replaces the table, and which
improvements turn this idea into the \ac{D3QN} agent that each \ac{V2V} pair uses.

\subsection{Deep Q-networks}
% Una red neuronal con pesos theta reemplaza la tabla de Q-learning; la pérdida de la DQN, el buffer de repetición y la red objetivo (Mnih et al.).
\label{sec:bg-dqn}

A \termdef{neural network} is a function with many adjustable parameters, its \termdef{weights}, which we
collect in a vector $\vtheta$. A network that takes the state as input and returns one estimate per
action, $Q(s, a; \vtheta)$, can replace the table, and what it learns in one state carries over to similar
states. Its layers alternate linear maps with a simple nonlinear function (in this thesis, the rectified
linear unit, $\max(0, x)$). The weights are trained by \termdef{gradient descent}: step by step, they move
in the direction that most reduces a \termdef{loss function}, computed on a small random sample of the
data called a \termdef{minibatch}. This thesis uses the Adam optimizer \cite{kingma+15adam}, a variant of
gradient descent.

A \ac{DQN} \cite{mnih+15dqn} trains such a network with the Q-learning target of
Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-qlearning}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\vtheta) = \E_{(s,a,r,s') \sim \mathcal{B}}\Bigl[ \bigl( y - Q(s, a; \vtheta) \bigr)^2 \Bigr],
  \qquad y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \vtheta^{-}) \label{eq:bg-dqn}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $(s, a, r, s')$ is a \termdef{transition}: a state, the action taken in it, the reward, and the next
    state;
  \item $\mathcal{B}$ is the \termdef{replay buffer}, a memory of recent transitions from which minibatches
    are drawn at random;
  \item $y$ is the target, and $\vtheta^{-}$ are the weights of the \termdef{target network}, a copy of the
    \termdef{online network} $\vtheta$ that is refreshed only every $C$ steps.
\end{wherelist}
The replay buffer breaks the correlation between consecutive transitions and lets the agent reuse each
experience; the target network keeps the target from moving with every update (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-dqn}).
Together, they make training stable \cite{mnih+15dqn}.

\subsection{The D3QN agent}
% Las mejoras que forman el agente de cada par: doble DQN, arquitectura de duelo, PER y pérdida de Huber, con una tabla resumen.

The agent of each \ac{V2V} pair, as in \citet{li+22fedmarl}, is a \ac{D3QN}: a \ac{DQN} with the
improvements below, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:bg-d3qn}.

\paragraph{Double DQN.} The maximum in Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-dqn} overestimates values, because it picks
precisely the estimates that random errors inflated. \termdef{Double DQN} \cite{vanhasselt+16double} lets
the online network choose the next action and the target network evaluate it:
\begin{equation}
  y = r + \gamma\, Q\Bigl(s',\; \argmax_{a'} Q(s', a'; \vtheta);\; \vtheta^{-}\Bigr) \label{eq:bg-double}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $\argmax_{a'} Q(s', a'; \vtheta)$ is the next action chosen by the online network, which the target
    network then evaluates.
\end{wherelist}

\paragraph{Dueling architecture.} In many states, all actions are worth about the same, and what matters
is how good the state itself is. The \termdef{dueling architecture} \cite{wang+16dueling} splits the
network into two streams, one for the value of the state, $V(s)$, and one for the \termdef{advantage} of
each action, $A(s, a)$, and combines them:
\begin{equation}
  Q(s, a) = V(s) + A(s, a) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|} \sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} A(s, a') \label{eq:bg-dueling}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $|\mathcal{A}|$ is the number of actions, so the last term is the average advantage; subtracting it
    makes the split between $V$ and $A$ unique.
\end{wherelist}
Since $V(s)$ is shared by all the actions, every update improves the estimate of all the actions in that
state.

\paragraph{Prioritized experience replay.} Instead of drawing transitions uniformly, \ac{PER}
\cite{schaul+16per} draws more often those with large \ac{TD} errors, which are the ones that teach the
most. Transition $i$ is drawn with probability proportional to $(|\delta_i| + \epsilon_{\mathrm{p}})^{\omega}$,
where $\delta_i$ is its latest \ac{TD} error, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{p}}$ is a small constant, and $\omega$ sets
how much the priority matters (the literature calls it $\alpha$, a letter already used here for fading).
Importance-sampling weights then scale down the effect of the transitions drawn more often, to correct the
bias this introduces.

\paragraph{Huber loss.} With the squared error of Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-dqn}, a single large error can move
the weights abruptly. The \termdef{Huber loss} \cite{huber64robust}, which this thesis uses instead, is
quadratic for errors up to 1 and linear beyond, so the size of each step stays bounded.

  \centering

    addresses.}
  \label{tab:bg-d3qn}
  \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
    \toprule
    Piece & Problem it addresses \\
    \midrule
    Replay buffer \cite{mnih+15dqn} & Consecutive transitions are correlated, and each experience would be

\begin{keyidea}
  A \acs{DQN} replaces the table of Q-learning with a neural network. A replay buffer and a target network
  make its training stable. Double \acs{DQN} corrects overestimation, the dueling architecture separates
  the value of a state from the value of each action, and prioritized replay trains more on the
  transitions that teach the most.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Multi-agent reinforcement learning}
% Qué cambia cuando aprenden varios agentes a la vez en el mismo entorno.
\label{sec:bg-marl}

So far, a single agent has been learning in an environment that does not learn. This section describes
what changes when several agents learn at the same time in the same environment.

\subsection{From one agent to many}
% Agentes que comparten un entorno, la acción conjunta y la formalización como Dec-POMDP.

In \ac{MARL}, several agents act in the same environment, and the outcome for each of them depends on what
all of them do \cite{gronauer+22marlsurvey,nguyen+20madrl}. The \ac{V2V} problem is an example: the
\ac{SINR} of a pair depends on the channels and powers chosen by every other pair. At each step, each agent
$k \in \Kset = \{1, \dots, K\}$ chooses its own action $a_k^t$, and the environment responds to the
\termdef{joint action} $\bm{a}^t = (a_1^t, \dots, a_K^t)$. Each agent sees only part of the state, its
\termdef{observation} $o_k^t$. When the agents cooperate toward a common reward under these conditions, the
problem is called a \ac{Dec-POMDP}.

\subsection{Why it is hard}
% Las dificultades del caso multiagente, sobre todo la no estacionariedad y la asignación de crédito, y la alineación frente a la facilidad de aprendizaje de la recompensa.

Besides the difficulties of a single agent, the multi-agent case adds partial observability and a number of
joint actions that grows exponentially with the number of agents: with 10 agents of 60 actions each, there
are $60^{10} \approx 6 \times 10^{17}$ \cite{gronauer+22marlsurvey,nguyen+20madrl}. Two further
difficulties matter most for this thesis:
\begin{description}
  \item[Non-stationarity.] While one agent learns, the others change how they act. From the point of view
    of each agent, the environment keeps changing its rules \cite{delafuente+24gametheory}.
  \item[Credit assignment.] When all the agents receive the same reward, none of them knows how much of it
    its own action caused. An agent may be rewarded for what another did, or punished for the mistake of
    another \cite{sunehag+18vdn}.
\end{description}

Credit assignment can be analyzed through two properties of the reward that each agent receives
\cite{wolpert+01collectives,agogino+08rewards}. \termdef{Alignment} is the degree to which improving the
reward of the agent also improves the objective of the system. \termdef{Learnability} is the degree to which
that reward depends on the actions of the agent rather than on those of the others.
Table~\ref{tab:bg-rewards} compares three kinds of reward. A \termdef{global reward}, the same for all the
agents, is aligned but hard to learn, and it gets harder as agents are added. A \termdef{local reward},
which measures only what concerns each agent, is easy to learn but may be poorly aligned. A
\termdef{difference reward}, which measures how much the performance of the system changes because the
agent is there, is designed to have both properties, but it requires evaluating the system without each
agent. The individual reward that this thesis proposes is a local reward; the proposed architecture
chapter discusses how much alignment it gives up.

  \centering

    \toprule

    \midrule

\subsection{Ways to train multiple agents}
% CTDE y DTE (clasificación de Amato), y dónde encaja el aprendizaje federado.

\citet{amato24cooperative} classifies cooperative methods by where training and execution happen. In
\ac{CTDE}, the agents use information from all of them while they learn, but each one acts only on its own
observation. In \ac{DTE}, each agent learns and acts on its own. The classic example is
\termdef{independent learners}, which run their own Q-learning and ignore that the others exist
\cite{tan93independent,claus+98dynamics}. They are simple and scale well, but they suffer the full effect
of non-stationarity. Federated learning lies in between: each agent still trains its own network from its
own experience, as in \ac{DTE}, but the agents periodically share and combine their weights.

\begin{keyidea}
  With many agents, each one sees only part of the environment, the environment changes while the others
  learn, and, if all receive the same reward, none knows how much of it its own actions caused. A reward
  specific to each agent is easier to learn, but it must stay aligned with the common objective.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Federated learning}
% La idea del aprendizaje federado y su aplicación al aprendizaje por refuerzo.
\label{sec:bg-fl}

This section presents federated learning, a way to train a shared model without moving the data, and its
application to reinforcement learning agents.

\subsection{The idea and the FedAvg algorithm}
% Entrenar sin mover los datos: copias locales del modelo, un servidor que promedia y la fórmula de FedAvg (McMahan et al).

In \ac{FL}, a model is trained with data spread across many participants, without the data leaving the
place where they were generated \cite{mcmahan+17fedavg,kairouz+21advances}. Each participant trains a local copy of the
model with its own data and sends only the weights to a server. The server combines the weights and sends
the result back.

The most widely used algorithm, \ac{FedAvg} \cite{mcmahan+17fedavg}, combines the weights with a weighted
average:
\begin{equation}
  \vtheta_{\mathrm{g}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{n}\, \vtheta_k , \qquad n = \sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k
  \label{eq:bg-fedavg}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $K$ is the number of participants;
  \item $\vtheta_k$ are the weights of the model of participant $k$ after its local training;
  \item $n_k$ is the amount of data with which participant $k$ trained, so a participant that trained with
    more data weighs more; $n$ is the total over all participants;
  \item $\vtheta_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the \termdef{global model}, which the server sends to every participant
    so that they continue training from it.
\end{wherelist}

For example, if two participants trained with $n_1 = 300$ and $n_2 = 100$ samples, the global model is
$0.75\,\vtheta_1 + 0.25\,\vtheta_2$.

Each repetition of this cycle is called a \termdef{round}. A round has four steps:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), nosep]
  \item the server sends the global model to the participants;
  \item each participant trains its copy with its own data;
  \item each participant sends its weights to the server;
  \item the server averages the weights with Equation~\eqref{eq:bg-fedavg}.
\end{enumerate}
The left side of Figure~\ref{fig:bg-fedavg-gossip}, in Section~\ref{sec:bg-dfl}, shows the communication
pattern of a round: every participant talks only with the server.

\subsection{Federated reinforcement learning}
% Aplicar el promedio federado a agentes de refuerzo; FRL horizontal, que es el caso que uso.

The same idea applied to reinforcement learning agents is called \ac{FRL}. Each agent learns from its own
experience, and the agents periodically average their networks \cite{qi+21frl}. When all the agents have the same kind
of states and actions but live different experiences, the setting is called \termdef{horizontal federated
reinforcement learning}. This is the case of this thesis: all the \ac{V2V} pairs observe the same kind of
information and choose among the same actions.

Sharing weights gives each agent part of what the others learned in situations it did not live through,
without sending its data \cite{qi+21frl,li+22fedmarl}. In the multi-agent setting, periodically averaging
the models also brings the policies of the agents closer to each other, which helps with non-stationarity
\cite{cheruiyot+25marlsurvey,xu+22mafrl}.

In the scheme of \citet{li+22fedmarl}, on which this thesis builds, the participants are the \ac{V2V} pairs,
each with its own \ac{D3QN} agent, and the server is the \ac{BS}, which averages the networks of all the
agents with \ac{FedAvg}.

\subsection{The cost of the central server}
% El servidor como punto único de falla, cuello de botella de la comunicación y entidad en la que todos deben confiar.

\ac{FedAvg} needs a server that receives and returns every model in every round. With $K$ participants,
the server receives $K$ models and sends the average back to all $K$, so it takes part in all $2K$
transfers of the round. This has three costs \cite{kairouz+21advances,ge+24dfl}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item the server is a single point of failure;
  \item it becomes the communication bottleneck as the number of participants grows;
  \item every participant must trust it.
\end{itemize}
If the server fails, or a participant loses contact with it, the learning of that participant stops
benefiting from the others.

\begin{keyidea}
  In federated learning, each participant trains with its own data and shares only the weights of its
  model. A central server averages the weights and sends the average back to everyone. The price is
  depending on that server.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Decentralized federated learning and gossip}
% Cómo aprender sin servidor, intercambiando modelos solo entre vecinos.
\label{sec:bg-dfl}

This section shows how participants can learn together without a server, by exchanging models only with
their neighbors.

\subsection{Communication graph}
% Nodos, aristas, vecinos y grado; grafos geométricos aleatorios como modelo de una red inalámbrica.

In \ac{DFL}, there is no server: the participants exchange models directly with some of the others
\cite{kairouz+21advances,ge+24dfl}. Who can talk with whom is described by a \termdef{graph}. Each
participant is a \termdef{node}, and two nodes are joined by an \termdef{edge} if they can communicate. The
\termdef{neighbors} of a node $k$ are the nodes joined to it. We denote them $\Nset_k$, and their number,
$|\Nset_k|$, is the \termdef{degree} of the node. A node with no neighbors is \termdef{isolated}. A graph is
\termdef{connected} if there is a path of edges between any two of its nodes.

In a wireless network, the graph comes from geometry: two nodes are neighbors if they are closer than a
certain distance. Such a graph is called a \termdef{random geometric graph}, and it is the model of a
wireless sensor network that \citet{boyd+06gossip} use. In this thesis, two \ac{V2V} pairs are neighbors if
their transmitters are at most \SI{300}{\meter} apart. Since vehicles move, the graph changes over time. In
the simulations of this thesis, positions are drawn anew at the start of each episode, so the graph changes
from one episode to the next.

Figure~\ref{fig:bg-fedavg-gossip} compares the two communication patterns. In \ac{FedAvg}, the graph is a
star with the server at its center. In decentralized learning, there is no center, and each node talks only
with its neighbors.

\subsection{Averaging with neighbors}
% Algoritmos de gossip: una ronda de promedio con los vecinos y cómo la estructura del grafo determina la velocidad de convergencia al consenso.

In a \termdef{gossip algorithm}, each node replaces its value with an average of its own value and those of
its neighbors \cite{boyd+06gossip}. With equal weights, one round is
\begin{equation}
  x_k \leftarrow \frac{1}{1 + |\Nset_k|} \Bigl( x_k + \sum_{j \in \Nset_k} x_j \Bigr)
  \quad \text{for every node } k \label{eq:bg-gossip}
\end{equation}
\begin{wherelist}
  \item $x_k$ is the value of node $k$ before the round; in federated learning, it is the vector of weights
    of its model;
  \item $\Nset_k$ is the set of neighbors of node $k$, and $|\Nset_k|$ is its degree.
\end{wherelist}
All the nodes average at the same time, with the values from before the round, and an isolated node keeps
its value.

If the graph is connected, repeating this rule brings all the nodes to a common value; the graph reaches
\termdef{consensus} \cite{olfatisaber+07consensus,boyd+06gossip}. This also holds if the graph changes from
round to round, as long as, over time, it connects all the nodes \cite{olfatisaber+07consensus}. How fast it
happens depends on how well connected the graph is. For example, on a chain of three nodes, 1--2--3, with
initial values 0, 0, and 9, one round gives 0, 3, and 4.5, and the next one 1.5, 2.5, and 3.75: the value of
node 3 reaches node 1, although the two never talk. The values converge to $18/7 \approx 2.57$, not to the
simple average of 3, because nodes with more neighbors weigh differently; reaching the exact average
requires special weights \cite{boyd+06gossip}. To share what has been learned, it is enough that the models
come closer to each other.

In \ac{FedAvg}, the graph is a star, and one round brings everyone to the average. Decentralized aggregation
gives up that speed in exchange for not depending on a central node.

\subsection{Learning and averaging at the same time}
% Combinar pasos de entrenamiento local con rondas de promedio (D-PSGD de Lian et al) y su costo de comunicación frente a la versión centralizada.

In decentralized learning, the nodes also train between rounds, each one with its own data.
\citet{lian+17dpsgd} analyzed this scheme for stochastic gradient descent, under the name D-PSGD: in each
iteration, every node averages its model with those of its neighbors and takes a gradient step on its own
data. The total computation is comparable to that of the centralized version, but the communication of the
busiest node depends on its number of neighbors, not on the total number of nodes, as it does for a central
server. On networks with low bandwidth or high latency, this made the decentralized version faster. This
thesis follows the same pattern, local training between rounds of averaging with neighbors;
Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} reviews other decentralized schemes.

\begin{keyidea}
  Without a server, each node averages its model with those of its neighbors. Round after round, what each
  node knows spreads through the graph until it reaches the others. Averaging everyone at once is no longer
  possible, but no node carries all the communication, and the failure of a single node does not stop the
  others.
\end{keyidea}
