# Summary: "Scalable Decentralized Multi-Agent Federated Reinforcement Learning: Challenges and Advances"

**Link:** [dx.doi.org/10.22161/eec.86.2](https://dx.doi.org/10.22161/eec.86.2) — *International Journal of Electrical, Electronics and Computers* (AI Publications/Infogain), Vol. 8, Issue 6, Nov–Dec 2023. Not on arXiv.
**Authors:** Praveen Kumar Myakala, Srikanth Kamatala — listed as independent researchers (Dallas, TX), no university affiliation given.
**Type:** Short narrative survey (8 content pages, 13 references). **No original experiments, no equations, no figures, no algorithm pseudocode** — every claim is qualitative and citation-backed, not measured.

## Important flag before anything else: this is not an empirical paper

The checklist (node 6) frames this paper as the source of "los datos concretos de degradación de rendimiento al escalar el número de agentes." That expectation does not hold up against the actual text. The paper's only quantitative-looking content is four qualitative comparison tables (Tables I–IV) that rate methods on ordinal scales — **High/Moderate/Low**, with no units, no benchmark, no dataset, no agent-count sweep, no plot. E.g., Table IV rates "Non-Stationary Agents" as having "High" effect on scalability with the parenthetical "(policies may diverge)" — that is a restated claim from the intro, not a measurement. There is no experiment anywhere in the paper that varies the number of agents and reports an outcome. If you need actual degradation curves for the node-6 table, this paper cannot supply them — `summary_frl_secure_incentive_icps.md` (Xu et al., a 9-vs-15-device sweep with real convergence plots) is the only paper in the current set that does.

## What the paper covers

Structured as five sections, each restating the same five challenges from a different angle rather than building on the previous one:

- **§II Scalability challenges** — five sources of difficulty in decentralized MA-FRL: (A) communication overhead/bandwidth (no central server to reduce inter-agent traffic, so volume grows with agent count), (B) non-stationarity (agents updating simultaneously shifts the environment other agents see), (C) heterogeneity (uneven compute/network/data across agents), (D) decentralized aggregation (no FedAvg-equivalent has emerged as a standard for the serverless case), (E) scalability-vs-privacy trade-off (DP/SMPC protect data but add overhead that itself hurts scalability).
- **§III Existing approaches**, split into communication-efficient methods (local model updates/FedAvg/FedProx, gradient compression/quantization, selective/gossip communication, asynchronous updates/FedAsync) and decentralized aggregation strategies (peer-to-peer/D-FL, blockchain-assisted FL, hierarchical FL, decentralized consensus/Nedic et al. 2008 distributed subgradient methods) — each just a paragraph naming the technique and citing one source, no depth on mechanism.
- **§IV Comparative analysis** — Tables II–IV, the ordinal-rating tables described above.
- **§V Open challenges** — communication protocol adaptivity, aggregation under dynamic (join/leave) participation, heterogeneity mitigation via clustering, non-stationarity stabilization, privacy/security, lack of standardized benchmarks (explicitly flagged: "a major limitation in current MA-FRL research is the lack of standardized benchmarking frameworks... hindering direct comparisons"), and a closing pitch for hybrid centralized-decentralized designs (a subset of agents as rotating "super-agents" — structurally the same idea as Martínez Beltrán et al.'s SDFL, see `summary_decentralized_fl_survey.md`, arrived at independently and with less formalism).
- **§VI Conclusion** — restates the above with no new content.

Notably, the paper **never once uses CTE/DTE/CTDE, Dec-POMDP, or any cooperative-MARL formalism** — despite covering "multi-agent" in its title, its multi-agent framing never goes deeper than "many independent learners causing non-stationarity for each other." It also never engages with game-theoretic or incentive-based multi-agent framings (contrast with Xu et al.'s Stackelberg formulation).

## Connection to the project

**Direct comparison with Xu et al. on the scaling-direction question (Node 6):** Xu et al.'s actual experiment (9 vs. 15 devices) found more agents *helped* — faster convergence, lower objective divergence, attributed to more parallel experience feeding the same FedAvg step. This paper's Table IV instead *asserts*, without evidence, that "Non-Stationary Agents" have a "High" negative effect on scalability ("policies may diverge"). These are not actually contradictory findings you need to reconcile — one is a measurement, the other is an unverified claim — but it's worth being precise about that distinction when building the node-6 table rather than treating both as equal-weight data points. The MARL-AD survey's joint-action-space curse-of-dimensionality (`summary_marl_ad_survey.md`) is a third, mechanistically different claim about *why* more agents could hurt (combinatorial action space growth in CTE/CTDE settings) that this paper doesn't engage with at all — it only discusses non-stationarity and communication overhead as scaling stressors, never joint-action-space size, because it never adopts the CTE/DTE/CTDE frame in the first place.

**Title overlap with the thesis is worth noting directly, and it's good news, not a scoop.** This paper's title — "Scalable Decentralized Multi-Agent Federated Reinforcement Learning" — is nearly identical to the thesis's own working title. Having read it fully: it stakes out no method, proposes no algorithm, runs no experiment, and does not adopt the CTE/DTE/CTDE vocabulary the advisor specifically asked about. It is a challenge-taxonomy op-ed, not prior art that forecloses a contribution — if anything, its own explicitly stated gap ("lack of standardized benchmarking frameworks... hindering direct comparisons") is a gap the thesis could be the one to fill: an actual implemented decentralized MARL+FRL system with a real agent-count sweep and measured degradation/improvement curve, which neither this paper nor (at the scale relevant to swarms/dense V2X) Xu et al. currently provide.

**For Node 7 (problem candidates):** the sharpest, most literature-grounded candidate this paper supports is exactly its own named gap — build and benchmark a decentralized MA-FRL system (gossip/consensus aggregation per `summary_decentralized_fl_survey.md`, applied to a CTDE-style critic per `summary_cooperative_marl_intro.md`, in a Dec-POMDP scenario per `summary_marl_ad_survey.md` or `summary_frl_secure_incentive_icps.md`) across a real agent-count sweep, and report what actually happens to convergence/divergence — something no paper reviewed so far (including this one) has done at more than a handful of agents (Xu et al.'s 9→15 is the widest sweep in the set, and even that is narrow).

**One useful citation, low-effort to add:** its reference [13] (Nedic, Olshevsky, Ozdaglar & Tsitsiklis, 2008, "Distributed subgradient methods and quantization effects") is a companion/predecessor to the same authors' 2009 paper already flagged as a suggested next-read in `summary_decentralized_fl_survey.md` — same lineage, worth grabbing both if pursuing the consensus/gossip aggregation angle.
