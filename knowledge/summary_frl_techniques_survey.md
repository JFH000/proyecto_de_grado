# Summary: "Federated Reinforcement Learning: Techniques, Applications, and Open Challenges"

**arXiv:** [2108.11887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11887)
**Authors:** Jiaju Qi, Qihao Zhou, Lei Lei, Kan Zheng (Univ. of Guelph, Canada / Beijing Univ. of Posts and Telecommunications, China)
**Type:** Survey/tutorial (published in *Intelligence & Robotics*), first dedicated FRL survey (no prior FRL-focused survey existed at time of writing)

## What the paper covers

The paper is a from-scratch tutorial + survey, structured to build up FRL from its two parent fields:

1. **Federated Learning primer** — formal FL definition (parties $\mathcal{F}_i$ with private datasets $\mathcal{D}_i$ jointly training $\mathcal{M}_{FED}$ without pooling data), the FedAvg algorithm (local gradient steps, then weighted-average aggregation $\bar{w}_g(t+1)=\sum_i \frac{n_i}{n}w_i(t+1)$), two architectures (**client-server**: central coordinator aggregates; **peer-to-peer**: no coordinator, direct pairwise/cyclic model exchange), and FL's three data-partition categories: **Horizontal FL** (HFL, same features/labels, different sample IDs — "same columns, different rows"), **Vertical FL** (VFL, same sample IDs, different features — "same rows, different columns"), and **Federated Transfer Learning** (FTL, neither aligned).

2. **RL primer** — standard MDP formalism (state/action/reward, return, discounting), value-based vs. policy-based vs. actor-critic methods, then DRL (DQN, DDPG, PPO, etc.) with a taxonomy table that includes a "multi-agent" row (MADDPG, COMA, coordinated multi-agent DQN, etc.) and a POMDP row (DRQN, RDPG).

3. **FRL itself (Section 4, the core section)** — FRL maps FL's (sample, feature, label) triple onto RL's (environment, state, action) triple, and mirrors FL's split into two categories, defined formally:
   - **HFRL**: $N$ agents each interact with their **own independent environment** $\mathcal{E}_i$, but all agents share aligned state and action spaces. Formally: $\mathcal{S}_i=\mathcal{S}_j,\ \mathcal{A}_i=\mathcal{A}_j,\ \mathcal{E}_i\ne\mathcal{E}_j$. Agents solve the *same kind of task* in *separate, non-interacting copies* of an environment (e.g., autonomous vehicles each driving their own city, or IoT devices each running their own local process) and periodically exchange **encrypted model parameters/gradients** (of actor and/or critic networks) via a coordinator (client-server) or directly (peer-to-peer) to accelerate learning and improve generalization to rare states, without ever sharing raw trajectories/observations. Six-step client-server procedure: init -> local SARS training -> encrypt+send local params -> coordinator aggregates -> sends back global model -> agents fuse into local model.
   - **VFRL**: $N$ agents interact with the **same shared global environment** $\mathcal{E}$, but each observes only a partial view $\mathcal{O}_i$ and may act in a different action space $\mathcal{A}_i$ — the union of all partial observations reconstructs the global state, and the union of all local action spaces reconstructs the global action space. Formally: $\mathcal{O}_i\ne\mathcal{O}_j,\ \mathcal{A}_i\ne\mathcal{A}_j,\ \mathcal{E}_i=\mathcal{E}_j=\mathcal{E},\ \bigcup_i\mathcal{O}_i=\mathcal{S},\ \bigcup_i\mathcal{A}_i=\mathcal{A}$. It's explicitly targeted at POMDP/Dec-POMDP settings. VFRL distinguishes **decision-oriented agents** (act and receive reward) from **support-oriented agents** (observe only — no action, no reward, but their observations still help others decide). Since raw observations can't be shared, agents exchange **encrypted mid-products** (partial Q-network/embedding outputs) that get aggregated into a shared value network, rather than exchanging model weights directly as in HFRL. The paper flags VFRL as far less mature/studied than HFRL.
   - The paper also notes a third bucket ("other FRL") for schemes that federate agents without cleanly fitting the HFL/VFL mold, e.g. reward-shaping-based federation (FRS) and multi-task federation via smoothed weight-averaging (MT-FedRL).

4. **Applications survey (Section 5)** — extensive cataloguing of ~25 FRL papers grouped by domain: edge computing (caching/offloading), communication networks (network slicing, RAN/O-RAN access control, V2X), control optimization (robot navigation, autonomous driving, IoT device control), attack/intrusion detection, and others (smart-home energy management, cooperative perception).

5. **Lessons learned + open issues (Sections 5.6, 6)** — aggregation-weighting design choices (reward-based, error-rate-based, batch-size-based weighting instead of plain sample-count weighting), the bidirectional FL<->RL relationship (FL improves RL's privacy/sample-efficiency; RL can also optimize FL's own participant-selection/resource-allocation), and six open research directions: HFRL convergence under non-IID environments, VFRL's no-reward-agent problem, communication cost, privacy/security (including FRL-specific reward-poisoning attacks), join/exit mechanism design, incentive mechanisms, and peer-to-peer cooperation protocols.

## Specific algorithms/methods worth flagging

- **FedRL** (Zhuo et al. 2020) — the paper's canonical VFRL example. Two agents (agent alpha: decision-oriented, agent beta: support-oriented, no policy/reward) share the same environment; each computes an encrypted local Q-network output, these feed a shared global MLP value network, and both local and shared networks update from the global output. Evaluated only on 2 agents / GridWorld & Text2Action — the paper itself flags this as a scalability and architectural-simplicity limitation, i.e. **VFRL currently has no proof of scaling past toy agent counts**.
- **HDRL** (Liu et al., RAN slicing, ref 9237167) — one of the only papers combining **both** HFRL and VFRL in one system: horizontal aggregation across base stations for same-service-type devices, plus vertical aggregation across different service types via a third encrypted party using Shapley-value-weighted feature aggregation. Useful as a concrete worked example of mixed HFRL/VFRL architecture.
- **LFRL** (Liu et al., cloud robot navigation) — HFRL + transfer learning combined, to handle the case where the shared/global model and each robot's local model have different network structures.
- **FedAvg** (McMahan et al. 2016) — the aggregation primitive nearly every surveyed HFRL paper builds on; weighting variants surveyed include reward-based, error-rate-based, and batch-size-based weights instead of plain sample-count weights.
- **ATT-MADDPG** (Mao et al., cited in the VFRL/MARL comparison, not itself FRL) — a centralized-critic-with-attention MARL method under POMDP, used by the authors as the point of contrast for explaining what VFRL does differently (see below).

## Connection to the thesis: HFRL/VFRL vs. CTDE

This paper is the most direct answer found so far to the advisor's question — "how transferable are CTDE/DTE techniques to a more federated regime?" — because Section 4.3 explicitly runs an MARL-vs.-VFRL comparison that maps almost one-to-one onto the CTDE question.

**HFRL vs. CTDE — where they overlap:** Both have a *centralized element that operates only during training*, coordinating information from otherwise-decentralized components, while execution/decision-making stays local. CTDE's central critic aggregates the joint observation/action tuple across agents sharing one environment; HFRL's coordinator aggregates each agent's local actor/critic parameters or gradients across agents each running their own environment copy. Both are periodic/round-based rather than continuous in practice (CTDE training rounds vs. HFRL's "predetermined interval or iteration round" aggregation).

**Where they diverge — this is the load-bearing difference:** CTDE assumes the central critic has *free, direct access to the raw joint state-action tuple* of a single shared environment at training time — the kind of unconstrained access the paper says FL/FRL is designed to prohibit. HFRL agents don't even share an environment (each local environment is independent), so there is no joint state to access in the first place — HFRL is structurally closer to privacy-preserving **parallel/distributed RL with FedAvg-style parameter averaging** than to any centralized-critic MARL scheme. **VFRL is the actually-relevant target for CTDE transfer**, since VFRL agents *do* share one environment (whose global state decomposes exactly like CTDE's joint observation) — but VFRL forbids sharing the raw partial observations/actions that a CTDE critic would need, replacing them with encrypted mid-products/embeddings aggregated into a shared value network (as in FedRL).

**Draft answer to "how transferable is CTDE to a federated regime":** CTDE transfers *conceptually* to VFRL, not to HFRL — VFRL is the FRL category with matching structure (one shared environment, agents' partial views compose the global state), whereas HFRL agents don't share an environment at all. But the transfer is not mechanical: CTDE's core enabling assumption — a critic with free access to the raw joint observation/action tuple — is precisely what FRL's privacy constraint rules out, forcing a redesign around encrypted/aggregated proxies (embeddings, masked Q-outputs) for that joint information. Per this survey, that redesign is immature: the only worked VFRL example (FedRL) handles just two agents with a shallow shared MLP, far below typical CTDE-MARL agent counts, so scaling a CTDE-style centralized critic into a privacy-preserving VFRL aggregator remains a largely open problem.
